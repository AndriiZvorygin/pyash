# Clause word-order comparison

## Scope

This note records clause-level subject, object, and verb order for language samples in the Sino-Pyash comparison set. `SOV` and `SVO` describe the relative order of a clause's core arguments and verb; they do not describe compound-word order, noun-phrase head order, or an entire language family without qualification.

“Basic,” “default,” “canonical,” and “neutral” order are source-specific analyses. They are not interchangeable measurements. Record the variety, clause type, information structure, and source whenever an order is used as evidence. Flexible order still has grammatical constraints and discourse effects.

## Recorded profiles

| Language or grouping | Current evidence summary | Scope and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Ket (Yeniseian)** | The Endangered Languages of Siberia description gives the most common patterns as subject–direct object–predicate and direct object–subject–predicate: SOV and OSV, both predicate-final. | This is a language-specific description of Ket. Ket is Yeniseian and is described there as practically the only living representative of that family; the order should not be presented as a survey of multiple living Yeniseian languages. [Ket language description](https://lingsib.iea.ras.ru/en/languages/ket.shtml) |
| **Lhasa Tibetan** | Default order is SOV; the order of preverbal constituents is described as flexible, and the paper describes Tibetan as verb-final. | Seth Cable's account explicitly limits its data to Lhasa Tibetan. This supports that variety, not every Tibetan variety or Written Tibetan. [Cable, *The Syntax of the Tibetan Correlative*](https://people.umass.edu/scable/papers/Tibetan-Correlative-Syntax.pdf) |
| **Northwest and Northeast Caucasian** | The cited Caucasus typology chapter reports a clear SOV preference, with flexible ordering and other orders admitted. | “North Caucasian” covers Northwest Caucasian and Northeast/Nakh-Daghestanian, not Kartvelian. The cited chapter discusses all three Caucasus families, including Kartvelian, so its generalization must not be misquoted as a language-by-language result for North Caucasian. Our sampled Abkhaz, Chechen, and Lezgian entries need their own variety-specific sources before making stronger claims. [Oxford chapter, *Information Structure in Languages of the Caucasus*](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/37095/chapter-abstract/323244579) |
| **Basque (Euskara)** | A neutral transitive order is Ergative–Dative–Absolutive–verb, consistent with SOV. | The Euskara Institute describes Basque as free-order at clause level, with permutations serving information structure. The cited source explicitly distinguishes neutral order from the other possible orders. [Euskara Institute, “The Sentence”](https://www.ehu.eus/eu/web/eins/the-sentence) |
| **Mandarin and Cantonese (Yue)** | SVO is a useful default description: bare verbs generally precede their objects. | Chinese languages also show SOV surface orders. Sybesma analyzes examples from Hong Kong Cantonese and Northern Mandarin and relates some verb–object variation to aspectual/complex verbal structures. Do not treat every preverbal object as the same construction or as evidence of an SOV basic order. [Sybesma (2021), “Voice and Little v and VO–OV Word-Order Variation in Chinese Languages”](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/synt.12211) |
| **Finnish (Uralic)** | Often described as canonical SVO, with flexible constituent order. | Elsi Kaiser's study labels Finnish a flexible word-order language with canonical SVO and studies how order affects anaphoric reference. It is one-language evidence, not a family-wide profile. [Kaiser (2004), “Word Order, Grammatical Function, and Referential Form”](https://septentrio.uit.no/index.php/nordlyd/article/view/28) |
| **Estonian (Uralic)** | Often classified as SVO, but clause order is flexible and information-structural. | Ehala reports V2 effects in main clauses, a high proportion of verb-final subordinate clauses, and an analysis involving S–I–O–V when auxiliary position is represented. This shows why the label alone does not capture its patterns. [Ehala (2006), “The Word Order of Estonian”](https://www.sejongjul.org/archive/view_article_pubreader?pid=jul-7-1-49) |
| **Hungarian (Uralic)** | SVO is a common basic-order description; all six S/V/O permutations are possible, with topic, focus, and other information-structural factors shaping order. | “Neutral order” and “basic order” are theory-dependent and debated. Treat Hungarian as flexible/discourse-configurational in this comparison, and cite the analysis used for any specific SVO or SOV claim. [Ronai and Xiang (2023), “Memory Versus Expectation”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10078164/) |
| **Uralic overall** | The family is mixed. Vilkuna's survey describes extensive variation in clause order; (S)OV is basic in the northeastern area and remains an alternative in languages where (S)VO prevails, with different discourse motivations. | This is a family-level overview, not a license to assign one order to every Uralic language. The chapter characterizes Proto-Uralic (S)OV as an assumed historical order; keep that reconstruction distinct from attested modern profiles. For Khanty, Mansi, and individual Samoyedic varieties, cite each language's description before assigning a specific profile. [Vilkuna (2022), “Word Order,” *The Oxford Guide to the Uralic Languages*](https://academic.oup.com/book/43672/chapter-abstract/366317448) |

## What this comparison supports

- Several selected samples—Ket, Lhasa Tibetan, Basque, and the sampled Caucasus families—have source-described SOV or verb-final defaults, with varying flexibility.
- Mandarin and Cantonese are commonly described as SVO, while allowing documented surface VO/OV variation.
- Uralic does not have one shared present-day clause order. Finnish, Estonian, and Hungarian are often described with SVO profiles, while northeastern Uralic languages retain (S)OV patterns; the exact analysis depends on the language, construction, and discourse context.
- An order found in a family-level overview is a hypothesis for checking against the specific wordlist varieties, not a substitute for their grammars.
- Clause order does not determine the head/modifier order of compounds. Keep this note separate from the Sino-Pyash semantic-compound rules and character-codebook scoring.
