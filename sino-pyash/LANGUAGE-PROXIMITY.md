# Language-family proximity to Chinese

This ranking is for choosing and labeling evidence in the Sino-Pyash character-codebook work. It does not claim that whole language families have one measurable sound distance from Chinese. Genealogical relationship, Chinese character-reading history, and phonetic similarity are different measurements.

## Genealogical proximity ranking

| Rank | Group | Relationship to Chinese | What the codebook may infer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Sinitic varieties | Chinese languages belong to the Sinitic family. Mandarin and other Sinitic varieties are the closest direct comparison set. | Use readings as direct variety evidence, with the variety and historical period identified. Do not collapse distinct readings into one undifferentiated “Chinese” value. |
| 2 | Other Sino-Tibetan languages | Sino-Tibetan is the widely used broader family classification containing Sinitic and many non-Sinitic branches. The placement and internal branching of Sinitic relative to other branches remain debated. | Use an individual cognate only when comparative evidence supports it. Do not treat a Sino-Tibetan language’s translated equivalent as a cognate or assign every branch an invented distance rank. |
| — | Other established families | No generally accepted genealogical ranking places Japonic, Koreanic, Austroasiatic, Kra-Dai, Hmong-Mien, or other established families at a specific distance from Chinese. Proposals for deeper relationships vary in support and remain disputed. | Keep these families unranked by ancestry. Record direct loans or other evidence for the individual word instead. |

This is the full defensible family-level ranking: Sinitic varieties are the direct comparisons; other Sino-Tibetan branches are the closest established broader-family relatives; there is no accepted finer ranking across unrelated families. It is deliberately not a numeric distance scale. Classification references place Sinitic within Sino-Tibetan while noting that the deeper affiliations and branching remain debated. [Glottolog: Sinitic](https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/sini1245) · [Glottolog: Sino-Tibetan](https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/sino1245) · [Handel, “The Classification of Chinese: Sinitic”](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199856336.013.0001)

## Evidence usefulness for Chinese character readings

This second ordering is about the kind of evidence, not family descent:

1. **Sinitic readings and reconstructions:** contemporary and historical readings, including Old and Middle Chinese reconstructions. Keep the variety, date, reconstruction system, and uncertainty with each record.
2. **Sino-Xenic reading traditions:** Sino-Japanese, Sino-Korean, and Sino-Vietnamese are historically transmitted Chinese character readings used within Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese. The host languages belong to Japonic, Koreanic, and Austroasiatic families; “Sino-Xenic” names the Chinese-derived reading/vocabulary traditions, not a language family. These data are direct evidence of historical Chinese loans adapted to each language’s sound system, not evidence that the host families descend from Chinese. [Eom, “2,200 Years of Language Contact between Korean and Chinese”](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199856336.013.0068) · [Obata, “Toward a Typology of the History of Loanword Prosody”](https://doi.org/10.5070/J7.67906)
3. **Documented non-Sinitic Sino-Tibetan cognates:** use comparative records such as STEDT where sound correspondences and etymological status are given. Cognate evidence can inform historical comparison; it does not automatically supply a character reading. [STEDT](https://stedt.johnblowe.com/node/124.html)
4. **Chinese loans in neighboring families:** use only identified loanwords and, where possible, distinguish borrowing periods and source varieties. Kra-Dai and Hmong-Mien have extensive Chinese contact histories, but that does not establish that either family is genealogically closer to Chinese. [Oxford Bibliographies: Kra-Dai](https://doi.org/10.1093/obo/9780199772810-0178) · [Mortensen, “Hmong-Mien Languages”](https://doi.org/10.1093/acrefore/9780199384655.013.341)
5. **Translation equivalents in other families:** use for semantic comparison or a separately documented cross-language phoneme-sampling procedure. They do not show how a Chinese character was pronounced or establish family closeness.

The order above is a starting order for evidence review, not a fixed score. A well-attested item-specific loan or cognate can be more informative than a broad family-level label. Store evidence with the item and let reviewers decide whether any algorithmic weighting is justified.

## Implication for source selection

For the character-to-code assignment, preserve the provenance of each candidate sound: Sinitic variety or reconstruction, Sino-Xenic reading tradition, comparative cognate, attested loan, or cross-language semantic/phoneme sample. Do not pool those as if they measured the same kind of closeness. Frequency rank, population coverage, and phoneme contribution can be reported alongside provenance, but each remains its own field until the reviewers approve a combined scoring rule.
