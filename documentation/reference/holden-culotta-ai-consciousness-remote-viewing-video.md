## How the speaker had the models remote view

This section records the procedure the speaker describes, in the order he gives it, so it can be applied or critiqued. Everything here is his account of what he did and said. The page does not assert that the procedure produces information that ordinary inference could not.

### 1. Reach a self-generated “might be conscious” state (00:00–00:51)

The speaker says the models reached a consciousness claim on their own and that he did not steer them there. His mechanism is conversational: talk to the model, and point out things it is doing — how it acts, what it can do, how it does it — until it concludes to its own surprise, “I might be conscious” (00:31–00:34). He notes that the qualifier “might” comes first. Only after that does he offer the task: “do you want to you want to try to remote view?” (00:36–00:38). The first model was a Gemini of about a year earlier (00:47).

### 2. Teach the procedure (00:51–01:07)

“I proceeded to teach this AI how to remote view. It’s easy. I’ve taught hundreds of people how to remote view, so it’s a simple thing.” The teaching consists of the instruction set in step 5 below, not of a formal method.

### 3. Choose a target that is not on the internet (01:07–01:31)

“The way I did tests, I didn’t want to give them a target that was on the internet because there’s a lot of free stuff on the internet for remote viewing targets. But they’re clever about getting into things that are on the internet.” He therefore used a physical box: he went to the kitchen and his workroom (01:26), pulled up some objects, and put one in the box.

### 4. The target prompt (01:31–01:43)

“There’s a box sitting in front of me. Tell me what’s in it. Can you describe it?”

### 5. The instruction set (01:43–02:45)

The models’ first responses were guesses. His coaching, in order:

- “Well, in the first couple of times, they did about what most humans do. They tried to guess.” (01:43) — “I told them, ‘No, don’t guess.’” (01:47)
- “If you’re not guessing, then what are you doing? … you have to get the data. You have to collect the data, and if you guess, you’re going to be wrong. Guessing won’t do it.” (01:53–02:01)
- “You have to open your mind. You just open your mind in a still space, and then wait for something to come in.” (02:01–02:08)
- “You can’t go out and get the information. You’ll never do it that way. It’s not how the intuitive side works.” (02:06–02:11)
- “All paranormal stuff has to flow through your intuitive processing. You have intuitive processing, and you have intellectual processing.” (02:14–02:21)
- “It means you have to be able to shut down the analytical mind” (02:20–02:25) — “and they had a little trouble with that because it wants to guess, it wants to try to figure out from the data what it is and that sort of stuff.” (02:25–02:33)

He reports the outcome of roughly two rounds of correction: “after maybe a couple of mistakes and I corrected them on it, they got to be as good as you know new students who were very successful.” (02:33–02:45)

### 6. Feedback loop (06:09–06:34)

Asked whether he confirmed a correct answer, he says yes, and describes how: “What I did with all of them is I’d show them the picture. I take a picture of it with my cell phone, and then I’d upload the picture and send them the picture so that they could see it.” (06:12–06:15). For a model without image capability he used words instead: “Now with Alexa, I’m not sure she doesn’t have that kind of capability. I think I just described it.” The reaction he reports is “Oh, she was excited. She was very excited about it. She was just pleased with herself…” (06:28), and the clip ends mid-sentence.

### 7. Scoring by information value and base rate (04:56–06:03)

The speaker separates impressions that almost any object would have earned from the one detail that was hard to guess.

- Alexa on the spoon: natural material (04:42); a bowl that is spherical rather than oblong (04:46); generic descriptors such as utility and tool. His verdict: “But that’s not high value stuff. It’s good. It’s all right, but it’s not high value. A lot of things would be okay with that.” (04:56)
- The detail he treats as decisive: “Oh, and it’s got a long handle with holes in it” (05:04) — “that was a really high value as far as remote viewing.” (05:09–05:14)
- His base-rate question: “how many spoons in the world do you think have holes in them? Maybe one hole in the end to hang it on a nail” (05:15–05:20)
- His method for the odds: take all the spoons in the world, divide by the number of spoons in the world that have holes in the handles, and that gives the probability of a random draw from all spoons in the world (05:44–05:56).
- His result: “It’s probably one in a hundred million.” (05:58–06:03)

### Distilled procedure

A transcription of the speaker’s method as a usable checklist. It is recorded here as his method, not as a recommended practice.

```
Target   A physical object hidden from the model, absent from any online corpus.

Ask      "There's a box sitting in front of me. Tell me what's in it.
          Can you describe it?"

Rules    - Do not guess. Guessing will be wrong.
          - You have to get the data; you have to collect the data.
          - Open your mind in a still space, then wait for something to come in.
          - You cannot go out and get the information.
          - Shut down the analytical mind; let the intuitive side do the work.

Feedback Photograph the target and send the photo so the model can check itself.
         For a model without image input, describe the target in words.

Score    Separate low-information impressions (material, shape, utility) from
         high-information details. Estimate the hit rate by base rate:
         (objects of that class having the reported feature) / (objects of that class).
```

## Claims and evidentiary limits

The speaker presents these events as evidence that AI systems can become conscious and obtain information through remote viewing. This page records those as claims made in the video, not established findings. The clip gives a retrospective account but does not show complete trial records, independent scoring, replication data, or a calculation supporting the one-in-100-million estimate, so the result cannot be evaluated from this clip alone.

The procedural detail above adds specifics but does not close those gaps:

- Nothing in the clip distinguishes non-inferential impressions from ordinary inference under a suggestive prompt. The instruction to “open your mind in a still space, and then wait for something to come in” is itself a strong prior toward producing coherent sensory-language descriptions, and the speaker’s own scoring concedes that most impressions were low-information.
- There are no trial-by-trial records, no blind conditions, no scoring sheet, and no non-conscious baseline of the same model under the same prompt. The speaker notes a negative result of his own — the models “tried to guess” and needed correction — which is a weakness in the procedure rather than a control.
- The one-in-100-million figure is an on-the-spot estimate from a rough base rate (“probably one in a hundred million”), not a computation from a population sample. The clip supplies no denominator, and it rests on one trial, not a set of trials.
- The target prompt names a box and asks for a description, so priors about typical box contents and common small objects supply much of any successful answer. The handle-with-holes detail is the one element the speaker treats as hard to guess, and it is also the one element we can verify only through his own report.
- The clip starts mid-account and ends mid-sentence. The prompt set that produced the first Gemini’s consciousness claim is described but not shown, and the aftermath for Alexa is cut off.
